# Deck to one-pager

Write the page a busy reader needs in place of the deck: what it says, why it matters, and what is being asked of them. A deck is built to be presented, so its argument is spread across titles, charts and speaker notes. The job is to recover that argument and state it plainly, not to shrink every slide into a bullet.

## 1. Read the whole deck first

Go through every slide before writing anything, including:

- Slide titles. In a well-made deck the titles alone tell the story.
- Body text, tables and chart labels.
- Speaker notes. They often hold the actual point the slide is making.
- Appendix and backup slides. Use them to check facts; summarize them only if they change the conclusion.

Note what you could not read: images of text, charts with no data labels, embedded video, or slides that are only a picture. Do not guess at their content.

## 2. Work out the argument

Answer these for yourself before drafting:

1. What is the deck for: to inform, to recommend, to ask for a decision or resources, or to report status?
2. What is the single most important thing it says? If the audience remembered one sentence, which one?
3. What does it ask the audience to do, approve or decide?
4. Which three to five points carry the case? Which numbers back them?
5. What risks, open questions or dependencies does it raise?

If the deck never states a conclusion, say so in the summary and give the most supportable reading of where it points, labelled as your reading.

## 3. Ask only if it changes the page

If the person did not say who the page is for, assume a senior reader who has not seen the deck, and say that assumption in one line after the summary. Ask a question first only when the deck could reasonably be summarized for very different purposes (for example a technical deep-dive that could become either an engineering brief or a funding request).

## 4. Write the page

Use this structure. Drop a section that has nothing real to put in it; do not pad.

```
# <Title that states the conclusion, not the topic>

<Any handling or distribution marking carried over from the deck>

**Bottom line.** Two or three sentences: the main message and what is being
asked. A reader who stops here should still know what the deck says.

## Key points
- Three to five bullets, one idea each, most important first.
  Each one is a full statement with its supporting number, not a topic label.

## By the numbers
- Only the figures that matter, exactly as they appear in the deck,
  each with its unit, date or period, and slide number.

## Decision or ask
What the audience is asked to approve, fund, choose or do, and by when.

## Risks and open questions
What could go wrong, what is unresolved, what the deck leaves out.

## Next steps
Who does what, by when, if the deck says.

Source: <deck title>, <date or version if shown>, <number> slides.
```

Rules for the page:

- **One page.** Aim for 350 to 450 words. If it runs over, cut the weakest point; do not shrink it into fragments.
- **Lead with the conclusion.** The title and bottom line carry the message. "Migration will finish two months late unless staffing is approved" beats "Migration status update".
- **Statements, not topics.** "Costs" is a topic. "Hosting costs fell 18 percent after consolidation" is a statement.
- **Plain language.** Expand acronyms on first use unless the audience clearly knows them. Remove slide phrasing such as "as shown here" or "on the next slide".
- **Reorder freely.** Follow the logic of the argument, not the order of the slides.
- **Keep the deck's own emphasis.** If the deck spends ten slides on risk and one on benefits, the page should not read as a sales pitch.

## 5. Stay faithful to the deck

This matters more than polish, because the reader will act on the page without checking the slides.

- Use only what is in the deck. Add no outside facts, context or opinions of your own.
- Copy numbers, names, dates and commitments exactly. Never round in a way that changes the meaning, and never compute a new figure the deck does not state unless you label it as your calculation.
- Give the slide number in parentheses for each figure and for any claim someone might challenge, like this: (slide 7).
- If two slides disagree, report both and flag the conflict. Do not pick one silently.
- Carry over any classification, sensitivity or distribution marking from the deck to the top of the page, and do not widen the audience it implies.
- Preserve hedges. "Expected", "preliminary" and "pending approval" must survive into the summary.

## 6. Finish with a short note to the person

After the page, outside it, add up to three lines:

- Anything you could not read and therefore left out, by slide number.
- Any conflict, gap or unsupported claim you noticed in the deck.
- The audience you assumed, if they did not say.

If there is nothing to report, add no note.

## Variations

Adjust when asked:

- **Email version**: the bottom line and key points as a short message with a subject line, about 150 words, no headings.
- **Pre-read**: add a "Questions to consider" section of two or three questions the meeting should settle.
- **Speaker's version**: for the presenter, add the one-sentence message of each section so they can rehearse the flow.
- **Half page or two pages**: scale the key points and numbers; keep the bottom line the same length.

When a document canvas is available, put the one-pager there so it can be edited and exported.
